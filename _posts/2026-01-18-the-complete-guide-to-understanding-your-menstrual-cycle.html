1. What Is the Menstrual Cycle?

The menstrual cycle is the series of changes that happens in the reproductive system during a cycle. It begins on the first day of menstrual bleeding and ends the day before the next period begins.

The cycle involves changes in hormones, the ovaries, and the lining of the uterus. These changes prepare the body for the possibility of pregnancy.

Every person's cycle can be different. Cycle length, bleeding duration, symptoms, and the timing of ovulation can vary.

2. How the Menstrual Cycle Works

The menstrual cycle is commonly divided into four phases:

  • Menstrual phase
  • Follicular phase
  • Ovulation
  • Luteal phase

These phases are connected, but they do not always occur on exactly the same calendar days for everyone.

3. The Four Phases of the Menstrual Cycle

Menstrual Phase

The menstrual phase is the part of the cycle when you have your period.

Menstrual bleeding occurs when the uterine lining is shed because pregnancy did not occur during the previous cycle.

Day 1 of your period is considered Day 1 of your menstrual cycle.

Some people experience cramps, tiredness, headaches, mood changes, bloating, or other symptoms during this phase, while others experience very few symptoms.

Follicular Phase

The follicular phase begins on the first day of your period and continues until ovulation. This means that the menstrual and follicular phases overlap at the beginning of the cycle.

During this phase, follicles in the ovaries develop. One follicle usually becomes dominant and prepares to release an egg.

Estrogen levels also generally rise during this part of the cycle, helping the uterine lining develop again after menstruation.

Ovulation

Ovulation is when an ovary releases an egg.

Many people are taught that ovulation happens on Day 14 of a 28-day cycle. However, this is only an example. The timing of ovulation can vary considerably between people and between cycles.

Luteal Phase

The luteal phase begins after ovulation and continues until the next period begins.

During this phase, progesterone generally rises and helps prepare the uterine lining in case pregnancy occurs.

If pregnancy does not occur, hormone levels eventually fall and the next menstrual period begins.

4. The Hormones Involved in the Menstrual Cycle

Several hormones work together to coordinate the menstrual cycle.

Estrogen

Estrogen plays an important role in the development of the uterine lining and the maturation of ovarian follicles.

Progesterone

Progesterone rises after ovulation and helps maintain the uterine lining during the second part of the cycle.

Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)

FSH helps stimulate the development of ovarian follicles during the earlier part of the cycle.

Luteinizing Hormone (LH)

LH is involved in the hormonal events that lead to ovulation.

These hormones interact with one another rather than working independently, which is why changes in one part of the cycle can affect other parts.

5. How Long Is a Menstrual Cycle?

There is no single cycle length that applies to everyone.

Many adult menstrual cycles fall within a broad range, and the length can naturally vary. Some cycles may be shorter or longer than others.

Instead of focusing only on whether your cycle matches a particular number, it can be useful to observe your own pattern over several cycles.

Tracking can help you see whether your cycle is generally consistent or whether there have been noticeable changes.

6. What Is Considered a Typical Cycle?

A typical menstrual cycle can look different from one person to another.

Some useful things to observe include:

  • How many days your cycle usually lasts
  • How long your period usually lasts
  • Whether your cycle is relatively predictable
  • Changes in bleeding patterns
  • Symptoms that regularly occur before or during your period
  • Changes compared with your previous cycles

Your personal baseline can be more informative than comparing your cycle with someone else's.

7. What Can Affect Your Menstrual Cycle?

Menstrual cycles can change for many reasons. Temporary changes do not necessarily mean that something is wrong.

Factors that may influence cycle timing or symptoms include:

  • Stress
  • Changes in sleep
  • Travel or major changes in routine
  • Changes in physical activity
  • Changes in body weight
  • Illness
  • Hormonal changes
  • Some medications
  • Life-stage changes

If you notice a persistent or significant change in your normal pattern, consider discussing it with a qualified healthcare professional.

8. How to Track Your Menstrual Cycle

Cycle tracking does not have to be complicated.

At the most basic level, record the first day of each period. From there, you can gradually record additional information that helps you understand your personal pattern.

You may choose to track:

  • First day of your period
  • Last day of bleeding
  • Cycle length
  • Bleeding intensity
  • Cramps or other symptoms
  • Mood changes
  • Energy levels
  • Other observations that seem relevant to you

9. Why Tracking Your Cycle Can Be Useful

Keeping a record of your cycles can help you become more familiar with your personal pattern.

For example, tracking may help you:

  • Know when your period may be approaching
  • Recognize your usual cycle length
  • Notice changes from one cycle to another
  • Keep a record to discuss with a healthcare professional
  • Better understand recurring symptoms

Cycle tracking is a tool for observation. It should not be treated as a diagnosis or a replacement for professional medical advice.

11. When Changes in Your Cycle May Need Medical Attention

Many cycle variations can happen for ordinary reasons. However, some changes deserve professional attention, especially when they are persistent, severe, or unusual for you.

Consider speaking with a qualified healthcare professional if you experience things such as:

  • Periods that repeatedly become very irregular
  • Periods that stop unexpectedly
  • Unusually heavy bleeding
  • Bleeding that lasts significantly longer than usual
  • Severe or worsening menstrual pain
  • Bleeding between periods that concerns you
  • A significant change in your normal cycle pattern

If you are concerned about your symptoms, a healthcare professional can help determine the appropriate next steps.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the first day of a menstrual cycle?

The first day of menstrual bleeding is generally considered Day 1 of the menstrual cycle.

Is every menstrual cycle 28 days?

No. A 28-day cycle is often used as an example, but real menstrual cycles vary in length. Your own cycle may also change from one month to another.

Do the menstrual and follicular phases overlap?

Yes. The follicular phase begins on the first day of menstruation and continues until ovulation, so the two phases overlap at the beginning of the cycle.

Does ovulation always happen on Day 14?

No. Day 14 is an example based on a 28-day cycle. The timing of ovulation can vary between people and between cycles.

Why should I track my menstrual cycle?

Tracking can help you understand your personal cycle pattern, anticipate your period, notice changes, and keep useful information for conversations with a healthcare professional.

Can cycle tracking diagnose a health condition?

No. Tracking can help you observe patterns, but it cannot diagnose a medical condition. If you have concerns about your cycle or symptoms, speak with a qualified healthcare professional.

Final Thoughts

Your menstrual cycle is a recurring process involving hormonal changes, the ovaries, and the uterus. Understanding the different phases can make it easier to recognize your own patterns and notice changes over time.

You do not need to have a perfectly predictable cycle to benefit from tracking. The goal is to understand your own baseline rather than compare yourself with an idealized 28-day cycle.
