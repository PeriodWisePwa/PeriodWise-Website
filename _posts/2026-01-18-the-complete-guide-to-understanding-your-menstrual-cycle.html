Whether you are learning about your body or simply want to keep better records of your periods, understanding the menstrual cycle is a useful place to start.

What Is the Menstrual Cycle?

The menstrual cycle is a series of changes that occur in the reproductive system. It begins on the first day of menstrual bleeding and ends on the day before the next period begins.

The cycle involves changes in:

  • The ovaries
  • The uterine lining
  • Hormone levels
  • Physical symptoms

Cycle length and symptoms can vary between people and can also change from one cycle to another.

How the Menstrual Cycle Works

The menstrual cycle is commonly divided into four connected phases:

  1. Menstrual phase
  2. Follicular phase
  3. Ovulation
  4. Luteal phase

The timing of these phases can vary. For example, ovulation does not always occur on Day 14. The commonly discussed 28-day cycle is an example rather than a rule that applies to everyone.

The Four Phases of the Menstrual Cycle

1. Menstrual Phase

The menstrual phase is the part of the cycle when menstrual bleeding occurs.

During the previous cycle, the uterine lining developed in preparation for a possible pregnancy. When pregnancy does not occur, hormonal changes lead to the shedding of the uterine lining through menstrual bleeding.

Some people may experience:

  • Cramps
  • Bloating
  • Tiredness
  • Headaches
  • Mood changes
  • Breast tenderness

Not everyone experiences the same symptoms. The first day of menstrual bleeding is considered Day 1 of a new cycle.

2. Follicular Phase

The follicular phase begins on the first day of menstruation and continues until ovulation. This means that the menstrual and follicular phases overlap at the beginning of the cycle.

During this phase, ovarian follicles develop. One follicle generally becomes dominant and continues developing toward ovulation.

Estrogen levels also rise during this part of the cycle and help support the development of the uterine lining.

The length of the follicular phase can vary between people and between cycles.

3. Ovulation

Ovulation is when an egg is released from an ovary.

Rather than assuming ovulation always happens on a particular calendar day, it is more useful to understand that its timing can vary.

Hormonal changes, including a rise in luteinizing hormone (LH), are involved in triggering ovulation.

4. Luteal Phase

The luteal phase begins after ovulation and continues until the next menstrual period.

After ovulation, the follicle that released the egg becomes the corpus luteum, which produces hormones including progesterone.

Progesterone helps prepare the uterine lining for a possible pregnancy.

Some people experience symptoms before their period, including:

  • Bloating
  • Breast tenderness
  • Mood changes
  • Food cravings
  • Fatigue
  • Headaches

If pregnancy does not occur, hormone levels eventually fall and menstruation begins again.

The Hormones Involved in the Menstrual Cycle

Several hormones work together to coordinate the menstrual cycle.

Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)

FSH helps support the development of ovarian follicles.

Estrogen

Estrogen plays an important role in the development of the uterine lining and other changes during the first part of the cycle.

Luteinizing Hormone (LH)

LH is involved in the hormonal events that lead to ovulation.

Progesterone

Progesterone rises after ovulation and helps support the uterine lining during the second part of the cycle.

How Long Is a Menstrual Cycle?

A menstrual cycle is measured from the first day of one period to the day before the next period begins.

There is no single cycle length that applies to everyone. Cycles can vary naturally between people and may also vary over time.

Instead of focusing only on a particular number of days, it can be useful to observe your own pattern over several cycles.

What Is Considered a Typical Cycle?

A menstrual cycle does not need to look exactly like someone else's to be typical for you.

Useful things to track include:

  • Cycle length
  • Number of bleeding days
  • Changes in bleeding
  • Cramps or other symptoms
  • Mood changes
  • Other recurring patterns

Understanding your personal baseline can help you notice significant or persistent changes.

What Can Affect Your Menstrual Cycle?

Many factors can influence cycle timing or symptoms.

Stress

Significant physical or emotional stress may affect the hormonal processes involved in the menstrual cycle.

Changes in Weight

Significant changes in body weight may influence reproductive hormone function and menstrual patterns.

Exercise

Very intense exercise combined with inadequate energy intake can affect menstrual function in some people.

Illness

Being unwell can temporarily coincide with changes in your cycle.

Sleep and Routine Changes

Major changes in sleep, routine, or travel may affect menstrual timing for some people.

Hormonal Contraception and Medications

Some forms of hormonal contraception and certain medications can change bleeding patterns and other aspects of the menstrual cycle.

Why Tracking Your Menstrual Cycle Can Be Useful

Cycle tracking is more than predicting when your next period may arrive.

Tracking may help you:

  • Understand your usual cycle length
  • Record the beginning and end of your periods
  • Recognize recurring symptoms
  • Notice changes in your usual pattern
  • Keep personal records to discuss with a healthcare professional when necessary

The goal is not to make every cycle fit a prediction. The goal is to learn your personal pattern.

What Should You Track?

You can keep cycle tracking simple.

At minimum, record:

  • The first day of each period
  • Your cycle length
  • The number of bleeding days

You may also record symptoms and observations such as cramps, mood changes, headaches, breast tenderness, bloating, or other recurring changes.

When Should You Talk to a Healthcare Professional?

Changes in your menstrual cycle can happen for many reasons, and not every variation means something is wrong.

However, consider speaking with a qualified healthcare professional if you experience concerning, severe, persistent, or unusual changes.

  • Periods that stop unexpectedly for an extended period
  • Very heavy menstrual bleeding
  • Severe or worsening menstrual pain
  • Bleeding between periods that concerns you
  • Major or persistent changes in your usual cycle pattern
  • Symptoms that significantly interfere with daily life

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my menstrual cycle length?

Start counting from the first day of one period and continue until the day before your next period begins.

Does everyone have a 28-day menstrual cycle?

No. A 28-day cycle is commonly used as an example, but menstrual cycles vary between people and can vary from one cycle to another.

Does ovulation always happen on Day 14?

No. Ovulation timing can vary depending on the individual and the cycle.

Can my cycle change from month to month?

Yes. Some variation can occur naturally, and factors such as stress, illness, medications, routine changes, and hormonal changes may influence menstrual patterns.

Why should I track my menstrual cycle?

Tracking can help you understand your personal cycle pattern, recognize changes, record symptoms, and keep useful information for conversations with a healthcare professional.

Final Thoughts

Your menstrual cycle is a recurring process involving your hormones, ovaries, uterus, and other changes throughout your body.

You do not need to force your cycle into someone else's pattern. Consistent tracking can help you understand what is typical for you and notice changes over time.

Start with the basics: record your periods, pay attention to recurring symptoms, and learn how your personal cycle behaves.
